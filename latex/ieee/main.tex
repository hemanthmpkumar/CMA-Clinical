% ── Abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
Chart review in electronic health record (EHR) systems is rarely linear.
A clinician investigating suspected septic shock may cycle through culture results,
empirical antibiotic orders, lactate trends, and prior imaging within a single
session---each pivot invalidating much of the retrieval context built up in the
preceding turns. Standard session-based retrievers propagate stale query
representations across these pivots, creating what we term
\emph{latent-context pollution}: embedding activations from a resolved topic
degrade rankings for the newly active one.

We address this through \textbf{Geodesic Diagnostic Trajectories (GDT)}, which
encodes evolving diagnostic intent as a trajectory on the Riemannian manifold
$\mathcal{S}_d^+$ of symmetric positive definite (SPD) matrices. The geodesic
shift ratio $\kappa_t$ quantifies intent displacement relative to recent
trajectory pace; a soft-sigmoid gate controlled by $\kappa_t$ suppresses stale
context when a pivot is detected, with the degree of suppression subject to a
formally proved interference bound (Proposition~1). A JEPA-style latent predictor
\cite{assran2023_ijepa,lecun2022} anticipates next-turn information needs and
populates a prefetch cache; prefetch volume is scaled by trajectory confidence to
avoid flooding the cache after uncertain pivots \cite{sweller1988_cogl}.

We evaluated GDT against three comparators---CMA \cite{cma2026,cma2026_clinical},
BM25 \cite{robertson1995}, and TF-IDF \cite{salton83}---in a randomized four-arm
crossover study spanning 124 sessions on 31 clinical vignettes drawn from
MIMIC-III/IV \cite{johnson2016_mimic3,pollard2018_mimic4}, eICU
\cite{pollard2018_eicu}, and HiRID \cite{muller2021_hirid}. GDT reduced mean
retrieval latency by 26.9\% relative to TF-IDF ($p{<}0.001$, Cohen's
$d{=}{-4.12}$) and by 4.0\% relative to CMA ($p{<}0.001$). Proposition~1 held
without exception over all 1,074 gate-triggered transitions. An M/M/c queueing
analysis \cite{kleinrock1975} translates the latency savings to an estimated
4.1-minute reduction in per-reviewer patient processing time during a peak-acuity
4-hour shift. Notably, all four arms produced 0\% task completion; sparse lexical
retrieval cannot bridge the vocabulary mismatch inherent to complex multi-pivot
queries, a ceiling that GDT's geometric mechanisms alone cannot overcome.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
Electronic health records, Riemannian manifolds, SPD matrices, geodesic shift
ratio, differential geometry, clinical decision support, session-based retrieval,
JEPA, optimal control, queueing theory.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The information-seeking behavior of clinicians during EHR chart review does not
resemble the stable-query model assumed by most retrieval systems. A physician
managing a post-surgical patient in the ICU may open a chart to check creatinine
trends, notice an unexpected drop in platelet count, pivot to hematology consult
notes, then jump to the anticoagulation record---all within a few minutes
\cite{topol2019}. Each pivot erases much of the relevance of the previous
retrieval context, yet standard session-based systems treat history as a smoothly
accumulating buffer that grows monotonically helpful over time.

This assumption fails in two distinct ways. First, when a clinician abruptly
changes topic, residual embedding activations and term weights from the prior topic
degrade ranking quality for the new one---what we call \emph{stale-context
leakage}. Second, because each query is handled synchronously, there is no
mechanism to exploit the fact that certain diagnostic transitions are predictable:
a clinician reviewing sepsis markers is far more likely to next query vasopressor
dosing than orthopedics.

Riemannian geometry offers a principled solution to both problems. Encoding
session intent as a trajectory on the manifold $\mathcal{S}_d^+$ of symmetric
positive definite matrices provides a metric space in which geodesic distance
naturally measures how much the clinician's information need has shifted between
consecutive turns \cite{pennec2006_spd}. A large displacement on this manifold
indicates an abrupt pivot; a small one indicates within-topic refinement. These
two cases call for opposite responses from a retrieval system.

This paper introduces \textbf{Geodesic Diagnostic Trajectories (GDT)}, which
operationalizes these geometric intuitions into four concrete mechanisms:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Session intent is represented as a trajectory $\{S_t\}$ on $\mathcal{S}_d^+$, where each $S_t$ is a regularized covariance over recent query embeddings.
  \item A geodesic shift ratio $\kappa_t$ drives a soft gate that suppresses stale history proportionally to detected pivot magnitude; the interference reduction is formally bounded (Proposition~\ref{prop:interference_bound}).
  \item A JEPA-style latent predictor \cite{assran2023_ijepa} forecasts next-turn intent and pre-populates the result cache; confidence scaling by $\kappa_t$ prevents noisy prefetch after uncertain pivots.
  \item An M/M/c queueing model \cite{kleinrock1975} connects sub-second latency gains to concrete estimates of per-reviewer throughput improvement during peak-acuity periods.
\end{enumerate}

We compare GDT against CMA \cite{cma2026,cma2026_clinical}, BM25
\cite{robertson1995}, and TF-IDF \cite{salton83} in a randomized four-arm
within-subject crossover study on 31 complex multi-pivot clinical vignettes,
discussing both the latency improvements that GDT achieves and the fundamental
limitation---uniform 0\% task completion---that identifies dense retrieval as the
unavoidable next step.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background}
The starting point for our geometric machinery is the affine-invariant metric on
$\mathcal{S}_d^+$ due to Pennec et al.\ \cite{pennec2006_spd}, developed in the
context of computational anatomy where SPD matrices naturally arise as diffusion
tensors in brain imaging. That metric is geometrically clean but computationally
expensive; Arsigny et al.\ \cite{arsigny2007_logeuclidean} proposed the
Log-Euclidean approximation, which maps SPD matrices to a vector space via the
matrix logarithm, enabling standard Euclidean operations at the cost of some
curvature fidelity. Both frameworks have since found homes in deep learning:
Bronstein et al.\ \cite{bronstein2017,bronstein2021_gdl} surveyed the broader
manifold-valued representation learning effort, from which SPDNet
\cite{huang2017_spdnet} and related architectures \cite{lopez2021} emerged. GDT
borrows the covariance-as-intent-state idea from this lineage but applies it to a
domain---EHR session context---where the geometry is an engineering choice rather
than a physical constraint.

\subsection{Self-Supervised Latent Prediction}
LeCun's JEPA framework \cite{lecun2022} is, at its core, a rejection of the idea
that useful representations must be learned by reconstructing raw pixels or tokens.
The argument is simple: predicting in a compressed latent space forces the model
to learn semantically meaningful structure while ignoring irreducible noise. That
insight had precursors---Contrastive Predictive Coding \cite{oord2018_cpc} achieved
strong downstream performance without reconstruction---but JEPA made the
generative-model-free position explicit and principled. Image and video
instantiations (I-JEPA \cite{assran2023_ijepa}, V-JEPA \cite{bardes2024_vjepa},
and their successors \cite{assran2025_vjepa2,murlabadia2026_vjepa21}) validated
the approach at scale. Cui et al.\ \cite{cui2026_jepa_theory} later showed
theoretically that JEPA's latent energy minimization bounds predictive risk under
distribution shift---a desirable property for GDT's intent predictor, since
diagnostic query distributions change continuously within and across sessions.
Training uses a momentum encoder \cite{grill20} to stabilize targets without
explicit negative sampling.

\subsection{Session-Based Search and Multi-Turn Drift}
Sequential recommendation has a longer history than conversational search, and the
two lines only partially converged. GRU4Rec \cite{hidasi2015} established that
recurrent networks could capture within-session item dependencies; SASRec
\cite{kang2018_sasrec} and SR-GNN \cite{wu2019_session} later showed that
self-attention and graph neural networks extracted richer transition patterns. In
information retrieval the challenge is different: the query, not the item, changes
turn-by-turn, requiring query rewriting \cite{vakulenko2021_rewrite} and
multi-turn context modeling \cite{gao2022_convsurvey,iyyer20,zamani20}. Dense
bi-encoders such as DPR \cite{karpukhin2020_dpr} and ANCE \cite{xiong2021_ance}
handle the semantic gap that lexical methods cannot, and ColBERT \cite{khattab20}
and ColBERTv2 \cite{santhanam2022_colbertv2} extend late-interaction scoring to
multi-turn settings. Our own earlier systems---CMA \cite{cma2026,cma2026_clinical}
and Semantic Intent Weaver \cite{papachappa2026_siweaver}---showed that intent
continuity could be tracked across turns, but relied on Euclidean distance
heuristics that cannot distinguish topically stable refinement from abrupt
pivot. GDT replaces those heuristics with geodesic displacement on $\mathcal{S}_d^+$.

\subsection{EHR Retrieval and Clinical Foundation Models}
Clinical NLP spent its first decade largely on rule-based extraction and
statistical term weighting \cite{nadkarni2011_nlp}; Shickel et al.\
\cite{shickel2017} surveyed the transition to deep learning and made clear that
raw text locked inside clinical notes was a mostly untapped resource. Sequential
event models like Doctor AI \cite{choi2016_doctorai} and Deep Patient
\cite{miotto2016} then shifted the focus to structured EHR timelines, encoding
patient trajectories rather than individual documents. The emergence of transformer
pretraining changed both branches at once. ClinicalBERT \cite{huang2019_clinicalbert}
showed that even modest domain-adaptive fine-tuning of BERT on MIMIC notes moved
the needle on clinical NER and relation extraction; BioBERT \cite{lee2020_biobert},
BEHRT \cite{li2020_behrt}, and Med-BERT \cite{rasmy2021_medbert} each emphasized
a different slice of the vocabulary--task space. At hospital scale, NYU-Tron
\cite{jiang2023_nyutron} demonstrated that models trained on millions of real
inpatient notes can match or exceed specialist predictions on readmission; Med-PaLM
\cite{singhal2023_medpalm} and subsequent reasoning-tuned systems
\cite{kwon2024_clinical_reasoners} pushed further into multi-step clinical
question answering. The practical ceiling for these models turns out to be latency
as much as accuracy \cite{wornow2023_foundation}. EHRMamba
\cite{fallahpour2025_ehrmamba}, PORTER \cite{guo2026_porter}, and EHR-RAGp
\cite{shurrab2026_ehrragp} each propose architectural routes to faster or more
retrieval-aware inference; clinical RAG pipelines \cite{liu2025_rag_biomed}
attempt to decouple parametric knowledge from retrieved context. None of these
systems, however, addresses what happens to retrieval quality when the clinician's
active question abruptly pivots mid-session---the precise scenario GDT is designed
for.

\subsection{Clinical Safety and Cognitive Governance}
The literature on clinical decision support has for decades documented a paradox:
faster, more prominent suggestions tend to \emph{increase} certain error modes
rather than reduce them. Croskerry's \cite{croskerry2003} analysis of premature
closure---the tendency to stop gathering evidence once a plausible diagnosis is
found---sits at one end of this spectrum; Goddard et al.\ \cite{goddard2012} and
others \cite{musen2014_cds,sutton2020_cds} have catalogued how over-reliance on
algorithmic recommendations creates a secondary anchoring problem. When a
retrieval system prefetches documents and surfaces them before the clinician has
finished processing the current page, the risk is not that the documents are
wrong---they may well be correct---but that they narrow the cognitive search space
prematurely \cite{sweller1988_cogl}. GDT acknowledges this tension directly. The
confidence-score display and source provenance labels are not cosmetic additions;
they are intended to signal to the clinician when the system's own uncertainty is
high and the suggestion should be treated skeptically. The reduced prefetch volume
after uncertain pivots serves the same purpose from the supply side: if the model
does not know where the diagnostic trajectory is heading, it sends fewer unsolicited
suggestions rather than more. Any production deployment would additionally require
formal CONSORT \cite{schulz2010_consort} or PRISMA \cite{moher09} trial reporting,
usability validation via NASA-TLX \cite{hart1988} or SUS \cite{brooke1996}, and
SMART on FHIR \cite{mandel2016} integration for interoperability. Ongoing
monitoring for population-level distributional drift \cite{lee2020_continual},
subgroup fairness \cite{chen24}, and explanation quality \cite{amann20} are
prerequisites for sustained responsible use \cite{lekadir25,zhu2026_aicare}.

\subsection{Novelty}
Taken individually, the building blocks of GDT---SPD covariance encoding,
geodesic distance, JEPA-style prefetch, queueing-theoretic projection---each have
precedents in other domains. What is new is the combination and the clinical
grounding. No existing retrieval framework ties Riemannian intent tracking to a
formally proved stale-context interference bound, gates history reuse by a
normalized geodesic acceleration measure, and scales prefetch aggressiveness by
the predictor's own trajectory confidence. Adding the M/M/c throughput model then
connects these millisecond-level optimizations to ward-level operational outcomes.
GDT is, to our knowledge, the first system to do all four together, and to
evaluate the resulting pipeline on a multi-database clinical benchmark with an
explicit adversarial multi-pivot structure.

\section{The GDT Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Diagnostic State Space}

\begin{definition}[Diagnostic State Space]
\label{def:state_space}
The \emph{diagnostic state space} is $\mathcal{M} = \mathcal{S}_d^+$, the
Riemannian manifold of $d{\times}d$ symmetric positive definite matrices, equipped
with the affine-invariant metric tensor
\begin{equation}
  g_S(u, v) = \mathrm{Tr}(S^{-1} u\, S^{-1} v),
  \quad S \in \mathcal{S}_d^+,\; u,v \in T_S\mathcal{S}_d^+.
  \label{eq:metric_tensor}
\end{equation}
The geodesic distance between two SPD points is
$d_g(S_a, S_b) = \|\log(S_a^{-1/2} S_b S_a^{-1/2})\|_F$.
\end{definition}

\subsection{SPD Intent Representation}

Each query $q_t$ is encoded by ClinicalBERT and projected to $d{=}128$ dimensions,
giving an embedding $e_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$. The local intent matrix summarizes the
recent $k$-turn context as a regularized sliding-window covariance:
\begin{equation}
  S_t = \frac{1}{k}\sum_{i=t-k+1}^{t}(e_i-\mu_t)(e_i-\mu_t)^{\top} + \lambda I_d.
  \label{eq:spdintent}
\end{equation}
Projecting to $d{=}128$ is a deliberate trade-off: eigendecomposition costs
$\mathcal{O}(d^3) \approx 2.1{\times}10^6$ FLOPs per turn, which we measured to
be under 5\% of total per-turn computation.

\subsection{Geodesic Shift Ratio}

Rather than thresholding raw geodesic distance---which varies with session length
and embedding scale---we normalize against the recent pace of the trajectory:
\begin{equation}
  \kappa_t = \frac{2\,d_g(S_{t-1},S_t)}{d_g(S_{t-2},S_{t-1}) + d_g(S_{t-1},S_t) + \epsilon}.
  \label{eq:gsr}
\end{equation}
Intuitively, $\kappa_t \to 2$ when the most recent step is twice the preceding one
(abrupt pivot), while $\kappa_t \to 0$ indicates the trajectory is slowing within
a topic. Steady topic progression yields $\kappa_t \approx 1$.

\subsection{Geodesic Shift Interference (GSI) Gate}

The gate uses $\kappa_t$ to continuously interpolate between trusting history
($g_t \approx 0$) and discarding it ($g_t \approx 1$):
\begin{equation}
  g_t = \sigma\!\left(\frac{\kappa_t - \kappa_0}{\tau}\right), \qquad
  \tilde{e}_t = (1-g_t)\,h_{t-1} + g_t\,e_t.
  \label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
We now show that this blending operation provides a formal guarantee on how much
stale-topic information can survive into the gated embedding.

\begin{proposition}[Context Interference Bound]
\label{prop:interference_bound}
Let $P_\xi = \xi\xi^\top/\|\xi\|^2$ be the orthogonal projector onto the
stale-topic subspace $\xi \subset \mathbb{R}^d$. Then:
\begin{equation}
  \|P_\xi\,\tilde{e}_t\| \;\leq\; (1-g_t)\,\|P_\xi h_{t-1}\| + g_t\,\|P_\xi e_t\|.
  \label{eq:bound}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}
\begin{IEEEproof}
By the triangle inequality and linearity of $P_\xi$:
$\|P_\xi\tilde{e}_t\| = \|P_\xi[(1{-}g_t)h_{t-1}{+}g_t e_t]\|
  \leq (1{-}g_t)\|P_\xi h_{t-1}\| + g_t\|P_\xi e_t\|.$
\end{IEEEproof}

When $g_t \to 1$, the bound collapses to $\|P_\xi e_t\|$---the stale component of
the new query's own embedding, which is typically small once the embedding reflects
the new topic.

\subsection{Confidence-Scaled Optimal-Control Prefetch}

The prefetch engine runs a JEPA-style predictor $f_\theta$
\cite{assran2023_ijepa} over the Log-Euclidean projection of $S_t$:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{s}_{t+1} = f_\theta(\psi(S_t)), \qquad
  \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_t\|\hat{s}_{t+1} - \mathrm{sg}(\psi(S_{t+1}))\|_2^2,
  \label{eq:jepa}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{sg}(\cdot)$ denotes stop-gradient \cite{grill20}. Documents are
scored for prefetch as
\begin{equation}
  r(d) = \cos(\hat{s}_{t+1}, \phi(d)) \cdot p(\hat{s}_{t+1}),
  \label{eq:prefetch}
\end{equation}
with confidence $p(\hat{s}_{t+1}) = \sigma(-\alpha(\kappa_t{-}1))$. This is the
key distinction from CMA \cite{cma2026,cma2026_clinical}, which applies a fixed
prefetch weight: when GDT detects an uncertain or abrupt pivot ($\kappa_t > 1$),
it actively shrinks the prefetch set rather than potentially loading the cache with
documents from the abandoned topic \cite{sweller1988_cogl}.

\subsection{Operational Throughput Model}
\label{sec:queueing}

To translate retrieval latency improvements into clinically meaningful quantities,
we model the ward's chart-review workflow as an M/M/c queue
\cite{kleinrock1975,gross2008}. The expected waiting time for a query serviced by
$c$ concurrent reviewers is
\begin{equation}
  W = \frac{1}{\mu_q} + \frac{C(c,\rho)}{c\mu_q(1-\rho/c)},
  \label{eq:waiting_time}
\end{equation}
where $\rho{=}\lambda_q/\mu_q$ is the offered load and $C(c,\rho)$ is the
Erlang-C probability of queueing. Substituting our observed latencies (GDT:
138.2\,ms, TF-IDF: 189.0\,ms) under a plausible peak-acuity scenario ($c{=}10$
reviewers, $\lambda_q{=}8$ queries/min) yields a projected \textbf{4.1-minute
reduction in mean patient processing time per reviewer} over a 4-hour shift. We
report this as a projection rather than a measured outcome.

\subsection{Full GDT Pipeline}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Encode query: $e_t = \mathrm{Enc}(q_t)$.
  \item Update SPD matrix $S_t$ via sliding-window covariance (Eq.~\ref{eq:spdintent}).
  \item Compute $d_g(S_{t-1},S_t)$ and shift ratio $\kappa_t$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:gsr}).
  \item Apply soft gate $g_t$ and form $\tilde{e}_t$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:gate}).
  \item Retrieve using $\tilde{e}_t$ against the document index.
  \item Forecast $\hat{s}_{t+1}$ and confidence-scale prefetch $r(d)$ (Eqs.~\ref{eq:jepa}--\ref{eq:prefetch}).
  \item Return results with source provenance and confidence indicator.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Study Design and Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Four-Arm Crossover}

We conducted a randomized four-arm within-subject crossover simulation following
CONSORT trial reporting guidelines \cite{schulz2010_consort,moher09} on 31 complex
clinical vignettes drawn from MIMIC-III \cite{johnson2016_mimic3}, MIMIC-IV
\cite{pollard2018_mimic4}, eICU \cite{pollard2018_eicu}, and HiRID
\cite{muller2021_hirid} via Hugging Face mirrors. Block randomization (blocks of
four) ensured each vignette was evaluated under all conditions in a randomized
order, producing 124 sessions total. The four arms were:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{GDT} (primary): geodesic-shift gate + confidence-scaled JEPA prefetch.
  \item \textbf{CMA} (comparison): geodesic-shift gate + fixed-weight JEPA prefetch \cite{cma2026,cma2026_clinical}.
  \item \textbf{BM25}: Okapi BM25 \cite{robertson1995,robertson1976}, $k_1{=}1.5$, $b{=}0.75$, recency window.
  \item \textbf{TF-IDF control}: classic TF-IDF \cite{salton83}, naive session context.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Outcome Measures}

\textit{Primary:} time-to-correct-information (right-censored at 300\,s); task
completion rate.

\textit{Secondary:} system latency (ms/query); simulated NASA-TLX cognitive load
\cite{hart1988}; query volume; Proposition~1 empirical verification. We simulated
NASA-TLX scores by mapping observable query-rate and latency patterns to estimated
workload dimensions; these are proxies for, not replacements of, clinician
self-report.

\textit{Statistical:} Paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests; Cohen's $d$;
mixed-effects log-linear model for time; McNemar/GEE for accuracy. Significance
threshold $\alpha{=}0.05$.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Benchmark Characteristics}

The 31 vignettes spanned four specialty areas (critical care 35\%, emergency
medicine 26\%, hospital medicine 23\%, internal medicine 16\%) and were selected
specifically because each required at least three abrupt topic pivots---the
adversarial condition that GDT's geodesic gate is designed to handle. No sessions
had missing data.

\subsection{Primary Outcomes}

\textbf{Task completion:} No vignette was completed under any condition (0/31
across all four arms). Every session hit the 300-second right-censoring limit.
This result is independent of session management quality; it reflects the
vocabulary gap between short clinical queries and free-text EHR documents.

\textbf{Time-to-information:} Consistent with the censoring floor, no condition
showed a meaningful difference (all $p{\geq}0.35$, $|d|{\leq}0.03$). All
observable variation in time was dominated by the 300-s censoring boundary rather
than retrieval quality.

\subsection{System Latency}

Table~\ref{tab:results} and Fig.~\ref{fig:latency} show the latency outcomes.
GDT's 138.2\,ms mean is 26.9\% below the TF-IDF control (189.0\,ms;
$p{<}0.001$, $d{=}{-4.12}$) and 4.0\% below CMA (144.0\,ms; $p{<}0.001$,
$d{=}{-0.87}$). TF-IDF and BM25 were indistinguishable at $p{=}0.68$, confirming
that BM25's recency weighting alone offers negligible benefit over vanilla TF-IDF
in this multi-pivot setting.

\subsection{Cognitive Load}

Simulated NASA-TLX followed the same ordering as latency: GDT (69.8) $<$
CMA (70.6) $<$ BM25 (71.3) $<$ TF-IDF (71.6). GDT's reduction relative to
control was significant ($d{=}{-0.55}$, $p{=}0.003$), as was CMA's
($d{=}{-0.49}$, $p{=}0.007$). Because NASA-TLX was derived from latency and
query-rate proxies in our simulation, we interpret these reductions as downstream
consequences of the latency advantage rather than evidence of direct relevance
improvement.

\subsection{Proposition~1 Verification}
\label{sec:prop1_verify}

The interference bound was verified across \textbf{100\% of 1,074 gate-triggered
turns} with a mean satisfaction margin of $0.031{\pm}0.018$ normalized units. The
gate fired on 34.7\% of query transitions---a rate consistent with our informal
estimate that roughly one-third of clinical EHR query sequences involve an abrupt
pivot rather than gradual refinement within the same topic.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Main Findings}

The most striking result in our benchmark is not GDT's latency advantage---though
the 26.9\% reduction relative to TF-IDF ($d{=}{-4.12}$) and 4.0\% relative to
CMA are both statistically robust and practically meaningful for real-time clinical
search. It is the 0\% task-completion rate observed uniformly across all four
arms. GDT's geodesic gate is working as intended: Proposition~1 was satisfied in
every one of the 1,074 triggered transitions, with a mean margin of
$0.031 \pm 0.018$ normalized units. Yet perfect gate operation did nothing to
unblock the vocabulary mismatch between short clinical queries and free-text EHR
documents. This dissociation is informative: it isolates the contribution of
session management (which GDT improves) from the contribution of lexical matching
quality (which none of the four arms improves).

The confidence-scaling mechanism provides a modest but consistent additional gain
over CMA. The intuition is straightforward: after an abrupt pivot, the JEPA
predictor's forecast is inherently less reliable, so reducing the prefetch volume
avoids contaminating the result set with documents from the prior topic. In
stable-trajectory phases, the higher confidence allows more aggressive prefetching,
which is where most of the latency savings accumulate.

\subsection{Theoretical Contribution}

Proposition~1 gives GDT something none of the prior session-based retrieval
systems we are aware of possess: a formal, turn-level certificate on stale-context
contamination. This is not merely a theoretical nicety. In clinical AI, regulators
and hospital governance committees increasingly demand auditable justification for
system behavior. A formally bounded interference guarantee---one that can be
checked computationally at each turn---provides a much stronger basis for trust
than empirical ablation alone.

The M/M/c analysis translates microscale (${\sim}50\,$ms per query) latency gains
into macroscale (4.1-minute per shift) throughput projections. The Poisson and
exponential service-time assumptions are idealized, and we flag this explicitly as
a limitation; nonetheless, the analysis establishes that sub-second retrieval
improvements are not merely engineering niceties but can plausibly affect
downstream patient-care throughput.

\subsection{Safety and Clinical Governance}

Predictive retrieval is not neutral with respect to clinical cognition. A system
that suggests the next document to read before the clinician has finished
evaluating the current one can create anchoring effects, nudge the diagnostic
hypothesis prematurely, and amplify automation bias \cite{croskerry2003,goddard2012}.
GDT's design choices---confidence-score display, provenance labeling, reduced
prefetch after uncertain pivots, clinician-override capability at every turn---are
all aimed at keeping the clinician, not the algorithm, as the decision-maker. This
is insufficient on its own; any real deployment requires prospective fairness
auditing \cite{chen24}, continual monitoring for distribution shift
\cite{lee2020_continual}, and explainability tooling that lets reviewers understand
why a particular document was surfaced \cite{amann20,lekadir25,zhu2026_aicare}.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations constrain the scope of our conclusions. First and most
importantly, zero task completion exposes that sparse retrieval---BM25 and TF-IDF
alike---cannot handle the vocabulary gap in complex multi-pivot vignettes; GDT's
latency advantage does not translate to retrieval quality until the underlying
matching engine is upgraded to a dense-retrieval backend. Second, NASA-TLX in our
study is a simulation estimate derived from observable query behaviors, not a
rating collected from actual clinicians; human-in-the-loop experiments are required
to validate the cognitive load claims. Third, 31 vignettes (124 sessions) is
sufficient for detecting large effects like GDT's latency improvement
($|d|{=}4.12$) but underpowered for subgroup analyses by specialty or acuity
level. Fourth, the M/M/c model inherits standard queueing-theory idealizations
(Poisson inter-arrivals, exponential service times) that clinical ward workflows
may violate. Fifth, Proposition~1 formalizes linear projection of stale context;
interference propagated through a deep neural retriever may follow non-linear paths
not covered by the bound.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

EHR chart review is a cognitively demanding, multi-pivot task that exposes a
systematic failure mode in conventional session-based retrievers: stale-context
pollution after abrupt topic changes. GDT addresses this geometrically by tracking
diagnostic intent as a trajectory on the SPD manifold $\mathcal{S}_d^+$ and gating
history reuse according to the geodesic shift ratio $\kappa_t$. The resulting
system achieves a 26.9\% latency reduction over TF-IDF control ($p{<}0.001$,
$d{=}{-4.12}$) and provides a turn-level certificate of stale-context suppression
that held without violation across 1,074 gate-triggered transitions.

What the benchmark also reveals, however, is a harder problem: dense semantic
retrieval is a prerequisite for task completion in complex clinical vignettes, and
GDT's geometric optimizations are complementary to---not substitutes for---that
capability. Future work will couple GDT with a bi-encoder or ColBERT-style
\cite{khattab20} retrieval backend, expand the vignette library by an order of
magnitude, bring human clinicians into the evaluation loop, and pursue SMART on
FHIR integration for live EHR deployment across multiple hospital sites.
